Summary: A practical lesson plan for introducing adjective clauses through familiar gastronomy contexts.

## Lesson overview

**Topic:** Adjective clauses with **who**, **which**, **that**, **where**, and **when**  
**Level:** Basic  
**Students:** Gastronomy students in their final semester of English  
**Suggested duration:** 60 minutes  
**Main objective:** By the end of the lesson, students will be able to use simple adjective clauses to describe people, food, objects, places, and moments related to gastronomy.

### Language objective

Students will produce sentences using these patterns:

- Person + **who/that** + verb
- Thing + **which/that** + verb
- Place + **where** + subject + verb
- Time or moment + **when** + subject + verb

### Materials

- The article [Adjective Clauses for Gastronomy Students](/blog/adjective-clauses-for-gastronomy-students.html)
- The students' coursebook
- Board and markers
- Paper or notebooks for the production activity
- Optional: pictures of chefs, dishes, kitchen tools, and restaurants

## 1. Warm-up: Describe your weekend

**Time:** 7 minutes  
**Interaction:** Teacher–students, then pairs

Start the class by asking students to describe their weekend using adjectives from the previous lesson.

Write a few questions on the board:

- How was your weekend?
- Was it busy, relaxing, fun, boring, or tiring?
- Was the food good?
- Was the restaurant busy?

Give a personal example first:

> My weekend was relaxing. I visited a small restaurant. The food was delicious. The server was friendly.

Ask students to share two or three sentences with a partner. Then invite a few students to share one sentence with the class. Write useful adjective sentences on the board, especially sentences about a person, thing, or place.

## 2. Transition: From adjectives to adjective clauses

**Time:** 5 minutes  
**Interaction:** Teacher–students

Use one sentence from the warm-up, such as:

> The restaurant was busy.

Remind students that **busy** is an adjective because it describes **the restaurant**. Then show that we sometimes need more than one adjective to identify or describe a noun.

Write these sentences on the board:

> The restaurant was busy. We ate at the restaurant.

Ask: “Can we put these ideas in one sentence?” Elicit ideas, and then write:

> The restaurant **where we ate** was busy.

Underline **where we ate**. Explain:

> **Busy** is one adjective. **Where we ate** is a group of words that also describes the restaurant. This group of words is an adjective clause.

Use one more quick example:

> The chef was friendly. The chef made our dinner.  
> The chef **who made our dinner** was friendly.

This transition connects the previous lesson with the new topic without introducing difficult terminology immediately.

## 3. Presentation: Build adjective clauses step by step

**Time:** 15 minutes  
**Interaction:** Teacher–students

Use the examples from [the Swipora adjective-clause article](/blog/adjective-clauses-for-gastronomy-students.html). Present one relative word at a time. Keep the questions simple: “Is it a person, a thing, a place, or a moment?”

### Step 1: *Who* for people

Write two sentences:

> The chef is my teacher.  
> He makes excellent bread.

Cross out **he**, join the sentences, and add **who**:

> The chef **who makes excellent bread** is my teacher.

Explain that **who** refers to a person. Show the pattern:

> Person + **who** + verb

### Step 2: *Which* for things

Use a familiar kitchen object:

> The knife is sharp.  
> It cuts the bread.

Combine the sentences:

> The knife **which cuts the bread** is sharp.

Explain that **which** refers to a thing, such as food, an ingredient, a utensil, or a machine.

### Step 3: *That* for people or things

Explain that **that** can replace **who** or **which** in the simple sentences they are studying.

> The cook **that prepares the sauce** is my classmate.  
> The sauce **that contains chili** is spicy.

Give students the short rule:

> People → **who** or **that**  
> Things → **which** or **that**

### Step 4: *Where* for places

Write:

> The kitchen is clean.  
> We practice in the kitchen.

Combine the ideas:

> The kitchen **where we practice** is clean.

Show the pattern:

> Place + **where** + subject + verb

### Step 5: *When* for times or moments

Use an example connected to restaurant service:

> The dinner rush is a busy moment.  
> The restaurant gets busy at that moment.

Combine the ideas:

> The dinner rush is the moment **when the restaurant gets busy**.

Show the pattern:

> Time or moment + **when** + subject + verb

### Quick concept check

Say a noun and ask students to call out the correct relative word.

- **chef** → who/that
- **blender** → which/that
- **restaurant** → where
- **lunchtime** → when
- **dessert** → which/that

Before moving on, remind students that the adjective clause must come directly after the noun it describes.

## 4. Controlled practice: Coursebook exercise 2

**Time:** 8 minutes  
**Interaction:** Individual work, then pairs

Ask students to complete exercise 2 in their books by joining the sentences with adjective clauses.

Before they begin, complete the first item together. Ask students to follow three steps:

1. Find the repeated person, thing, place, or moment.
2. Choose **who**, **which**, **that**, **where**, or **when**.
3. Put the adjective clause immediately after the noun it describes.

Students work individually first. Then they compare their answers with a partner. Monitor and help them notice repeated subjects such as *the chef who he cooks*.

Check the answers as a class. Ask students to explain each choice with one word: **person**, **thing**, **place**, or **moment**.

## 5. Controlled practice: Coursebook exercise 3

**Time:** 7 minutes  
**Interaction:** Individual work, then whole class

Ask students to complete exercise 3 by filling in each blank with the correct relative word.

Keep this guide visible on the board:

- person → **who/that**
- thing → **which/that**
- place → **where**
- time or moment → **when**

Students compare answers in pairs before the class check. When reviewing, do not ask only for the answer. Ask a short follow-up question:

> Why **where**?  
> Because *restaurant* is a place.

## 6. Production: Create a restaurant concept

**Time:** 13 minutes  
**Interaction:** Groups of three or four

Instead of describing their town, students create a simple restaurant concept. This gives every group different ideas and lets students use vocabulary related to their degree.

Tell each group to imagine that they will open a restaurant. They must choose:

- a restaurant name;
- one chef or employee;
- one special dish;
- one useful kitchen tool;
- one special area or location;
- one important day, time, or service moment.

Each group writes at least five sentences. Display these sentence starters:

- Our chef is a person **who** ...
- Our special dish is a dish **that/which** ...
- We use a tool **that/which** ...
- Our restaurant has a place **where** ...
- Our busiest moment is the time **when** ...

### Production word bank

Because this may be the students' first time seeing these words in English, review their meanings before the activity.

- **chef's knife** — cuchillo de chef
- **cutting board** — tabla de cortar
- **whisk** — batidor de globo
- **spatula** — espátula
- **ladle** — cucharón
- **tongs** — pinzas
- **mixing bowl** — tazón para mezclar
- **pot** — olla
- **pan** — sartén
- **stove** — estufa
- **oven** — horno
- **blender** — licuadora

### Kitchen-item picture bank

Ask students to point to an item, say its name, and use it in an adjective clause before they begin writing. For example: *A whisk is a tool that mixes ingredients.*

Example:

> Our restaurant is called Green Table.  
> Our chef is a person **who makes fresh pasta**.  
> Our special dish is a pasta **that contains local cheese**.  
> We use a machine **which makes pasta quickly**.  
> We have a garden **where customers eat outside**.  
> Friday night is the time **when the restaurant gets very busy**.

Groups give a one-minute presentation. While listening, the other students choose the restaurant they would most like to visit. They must give one reason at the end:

> I want to visit Green Table because it has a garden where customers eat outside.

This small choice gives students a reason to listen to the other groups.

## 7. Feedback and exit ticket

**Time:** 5 minutes  
**Interaction:** Whole class, then individual work

After the presentations, write two or three anonymous examples from your monitoring on the board. Include one successful sentence and one sentence that needs correction. Ask the class to correct the sentences together.

To finish, ask each student to write one new sentence about gastronomy on a small piece of paper or in their notebook. Assign the relative word by row or group so that the class practices all five options:

- Group 1: **who**
- Group 2: **which**
- Group 3: **that**
- Group 4: **where**
- Group 5: **when**

Collect or quickly check the sentences before students leave.

## Anticipated problems and solutions

### Students repeat the subject

Students may write *the chef who he makes bread*.

Remind them that **who**, **which**, and **that** replace the subject in this pattern:

> :x: The chef who he makes bread  
> :white_check_mark: The chef who makes bread

### Students confuse people and things

Keep the person–thing–place–moment guide on the board throughout the lesson. Ask students to identify the noun before choosing a relative word.

### Students try to translate every word

Encourage students to communicate one simple idea instead of translating a long Spanish sentence. Let them use the displayed sentence starters during the production task.

### Students need more support during production

Give them a word bank with familiar verbs and adjectives:

- **Verbs:** cooks, makes, prepares, cuts, serves, contains, works
- **Adjectives:** fresh, hot, cold, spicy, sweet, clean, busy, delicious

If necessary, require only four sentences and let the group choose between **which** and **that** for the thing sentence.

## Optional homework

Students write a short recommendation for a real or imaginary restaurant. They should include four adjective clauses: one about a person, one about a dish or tool, one about a place, and one about a time or moment.
