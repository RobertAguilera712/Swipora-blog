Summary: A practical lesson plan for teaching have to, must, and should through a restaurant story and kitchen situations.

## Lesson overview

**Topic:** Advice and obligations with **have to**, **must**, and **should**  
**Level:** Basic  
**Students:** 30 gastronomy students  
**Suggested duration:** 60 minutes  
**Main objective:** By the end of the lesson, students will be able to use **have to** for requirements, **must** for strong rules, and **should** for advice in familiar restaurant and kitchen situations.

### Language objectives

Students will be able to:

- use **have to/has to + base verb** for requirements and necessary actions;
- use **must/mustn't + base verb** for strong rules and prohibitions;
- use **should/shouldn't + base verb** for advice;
- explain the difference between **mustn't** and **don't have to**;
- produce simple sentences about working safely and successfully in a kitchen.

### Materials

- The article [Advice and Obligations](/blog/advice-and-obligations.html)
- The students' coursebook
- Board and markers
- Six sheets of paper, one for each production group
- Optional: a picture of a busy or disorganized restaurant kitchen

## 1. Warm-up: Talk about the weekend

**Time:** 7 minutes  
**Interaction:** Teacher–students, then pairs

Begin with the same familiar routine that worked in the previous class. Ask students to describe their weekend using adjectives.

Write these questions on the board:

- How was your weekend?
- Was it busy, relaxing, fun, boring, or tiring?
- Did you cook or eat something good?
- Did you visit a restaurant?

Model a short answer:

> My weekend was busy but fun. I visited a restaurant on Saturday. The food was delicious, but the service was slow.

Give students one minute to think. Then ask them to tell a partner two sentences about their weekend. Invite three or four students to share one sentence with the class.

Do not correct every error during this stage. The purpose is to reactivate English and prepare students to listen to the restaurant story.

## 2. Introduction: A restaurant story

**Time:** 7 minutes  
**Interaction:** Teacher–students

Tell students a short story about a restaurant experience. Keep it personal and conversational, as this approach held their attention successfully in the previous lesson.

### Suggested story

> On Saturday, I went to a new restaurant. The restaurant was beautiful, but the kitchen looked very busy. One cook was not wearing a hairnet. Another cook touched food without washing his hands. The server was friendly, but she forgot my drink. The food tasted good, but it arrived cold. I started thinking about the restaurant staff. What are their obligations? What advice can we give them?

Tell the story naturally before displaying any grammar. Use gestures or draw simple pictures on the board for **hairnet**, **wash hands**, **cold food**, and **forgot my drink**.

After the story, ask quick questions that the whole class can answer:

- Does a cook have to wash their hands?
- Must kitchen staff follow food-safety rules?
- Should the server write down the order?
- Should food arrive cold?

Accept short answers at first. Then reformulate their ideas as complete sentences and write three examples on the board:

> Kitchen staff **have to wash** their hands.  
> Cooks **must follow** food-safety rules.  
> The server **should write down** the order.

Circle **have to**, **must**, and **should**. Underline the base verbs.

## 3. Explanation of the grammar

**Time:** 15 minutes  
**Interaction:** Teacher–students with whole-class checks

Use the examples and color coding from the [Swipora article](/blog/advice-and-obligations.html). Explain one form at a time. Teacher talk is appropriate here, but pause frequently for quick checks that all 30 students can answer together.

### Have to: a requirement or necessary action

Explain that **have to** is used when a responsibility, schedule, workplace, or external rule makes an action necessary.

Write the structure:

> Subject + **have to/has to** + base verb

Use gastronomy examples:

> I **have to wear** a uniform.  
> The chef **has to check** the ingredients.  
> We **have to arrive** at seven.

Remind students:

> I/you/we/they → **have to**  
> he/she/it → **has to**

For the negative, explain that **don't have to** means an action is not necessary:

> We **don't have to work** on Sunday.

Quick check: ask, “Can we work on Sunday if we want to?” The answer is **yes** because the action is optional.

### Must: a strong rule or obligation

Explain that **must** is common in strong rules, instructions, and safety requirements. It does not change with the subject.

Write the structure:

> Subject + **must** + base verb

Use examples:

> Kitchen staff **must wash** their hands.  
> Students **must follow** the safety rules.

Then introduce **mustn't** as a prohibition:

> You **mustn't touch** a hot pan without protection.

Quick check: ask, “Is touching the pan optional?” The answer is **no**. It is prohibited.

Contrast the two negative forms clearly:

> You **mustn't enter** the kitchen. = It is prohibited.  
> You **don't have to enter** the kitchen. = It is not necessary.

### Should: advice

Explain that **should** communicates a recommendation or a good idea, not an obligation. It does not change with the subject.

Write the structure:

> Subject + **should/shouldn't** + base verb

Use examples:

> You **should wear** comfortable shoes.  
> You **shouldn't skip** breakfast.

Quick check: ask, “Is this a rule or a good idea?” Students answer **a good idea**.

### Final concept check

Read each situation. Students show one, two, or three fingers:

1. **have to** — requirement or necessity
2. **must** — strong rule or prohibition
3. **should** — advice

Situations:

- A restaurant employee is required to wear a uniform. (**have to**)
- A cook gives a friend advice about comfortable shoes. (**should**)
- A sign says that students cannot use a machine without permission. (**mustn't**)
- The class starts at eight, so students need to arrive before eight. (**have to**)

This check involves the complete class without requiring students to speak one at a time.

## 4. Book exercise 2: Have To

**Time:** 8 minutes  
**Interaction:** Individual work, pair check, whole-class feedback

Ask students to complete exercise 2 by filling in the blanks with the correct form of **have to**.

Before they begin, remind them to follow these steps:

1. Find the subject.
2. Decide between **have to** and **has to**.
3. Look for an auxiliary such as **do**, **does**, **don't**, or **doesn't**.
4. Use the base form of the main verb.

Complete the first item together. Students then work individually for four minutes and compare answers with the person next to them for two minutes.

Check the answers quickly as a class. Ask different sections of the room to read different answers so participation is distributed without taking too much time.

## 5. Book exercise 4: Should and Must

**Time:** 8 minutes  
**Interaction:** Individual work, pair check, whole-class feedback

Ask students to complete exercise 4 by choosing words from the box for each blank.

Before they begin, write this guide on the board:

- strong rule or obligation → **must**
- prohibition → **mustn't**
- advice or good idea → **should**
- advice against an action → **shouldn't**

Students work individually and then compare answers in pairs. During feedback, ask one short question after each answer:

> Is it a rule or advice?  
> Is the action prohibited or only a bad idea?

These questions check meaning instead of only checking the word in the blank.

## 6. Production: New kitchen staff orientation

**Time:** 12 minutes  
**Interaction:** Six groups of five

Tell students that a new employee will begin working in their restaurant tomorrow. Each group must prepare a short orientation for that employee.

Divide the class into six groups of five. Assign a different restaurant or kitchen context to each group:

1. a bakery;
2. a seafood restaurant;
3. a school cafeteria;
4. a fine-dining restaurant;
5. a food truck;
6. a hotel kitchen.

Each group writes six sentences:

- two workplace requirements with **have to/has to**;
- one important safety rule with **must**;
- one prohibition with **mustn't**;
- one recommendation with **should**;
- one recommendation against an action with **shouldn't**.

Display these sentence starters:

> New employees have to ...  
> The chef has to ...  
> Kitchen staff must ...  
> You mustn't ...  
> You should ...  
> You shouldn't ...

### Kitchen word bank

**Actions:** wash, clean, check, cut, cook, store, wear, arrive, taste, touch, serve, label  
**Items:** hands, knives, cutting boards, ingredients, food, uniform, apron, gloves, hairnet, refrigerator, oven, stove  
**Useful complements:** before cooking, after using it, every day, at the correct temperature, on time, without permission

Example:

> New employees **have to wear** a clean uniform.  
> The chef **has to check** the ingredients.  
> Kitchen staff **must wash** their hands before cooking.  
> You **mustn't touch** food with dirty hands.  
> You **should wear** comfortable shoes.  
> You **shouldn't leave** knives near the edge of a table.

### Managing the activity with 30 students

Give each student a simple role so everyone has a task:

- **reader:** reads the instructions;
- **idea person:** suggests kitchen rules;
- **writer:** records the sentences;
- **grammar checker:** checks the target expressions and base verbs;
- **speaker:** presents the final sentences.

Tell students that you may choose a different person to read one sentence after the main speaker finishes. This encourages everyone to understand the group's work.

Give groups seven minutes to write. Then each group presents its best two sentences: one obligation and one piece of advice. While listening, the class shows an **O** with their arms for obligation or an **A** for advice. This keeps all students involved while keeping presentations short.

## 7. Feedback and exit ticket

**Time:** 3 minutes  
**Interaction:** Whole class, then individual work

Write one successful sentence and one sentence that needs correction on the board. Do not identify the groups. Ask the class to correct the second sentence together.

Before leaving, each student completes one sentence on paper or in their notebook:

> In a professional kitchen, I __________ because __________.

Students choose **have to**, **must**, **mustn't**, **should**, or **shouldn't**. Read a few responses or collect them to check whether students understand the difference between advice and obligation.

## Anticipated problems and solutions

### Students use *has to* after *doesn't*

Write and keep this contrast on the board:

> She **has to** clean the kitchen.  
> She **doesn't have to** clean the kitchen.

Explain that **doesn't** carries the change, so we use **have to** after it.

### Students add *to* after *must* or *should*

Cross out **to** and point back to the structures on the board:

> :x: must to wash  
> :white_check_mark: must wash  
> :x: should to wear  
> :white_check_mark: should wear

### Students confuse *mustn't* and *don't have to*

Use two gestures consistently:

- **mustn't:** make an X with both arms to show prohibition;
- **don't have to:** open both hands to show that the action is optional.

### A few students dominate the group activity

Assign the five roles before students begin. Tell groups that every member should be ready to read and explain one sentence.

### Students need vocabulary during production

Keep the kitchen word bank visible. Encourage simple, correct sentences instead of long ideas translated directly from Spanish.

## Optional homework

Students imagine that they are training a new cook. They write six sentences: two with **have to**, two with **must/mustn't**, and two with **should/shouldn't**.
