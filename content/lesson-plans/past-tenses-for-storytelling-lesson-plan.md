Title: Lesson Plan: Past Tenses for Storytelling
Date: 2026-10-06
Category: Lesson Plans
Tags: Lesson Plan, Simple Past, Past Progressive, Past Perfect, Storytelling, Gastronomy
Status: Draft
Summary: A storytelling lesson in which gastronomy students use three past tenses to share an entertaining kitchen story.

## Lesson overview

**Topic:** Simple past, past progressive, and past perfect for storytelling  
**Level:** Basic  
**Students:** Approximately 30 gastronomy students  
**Suggested duration:** 60 minutes  
**Main objective:** By the end of the lesson, students will be able to combine the three past tenses to tell a short, organized story.

### Language objectives

Students will use:

- the **simple past** for the main events in a story;
- the **past progressive** to establish the scene or describe an action in progress;
- the **past perfect** to explain an event that happened before another past event;
- **when**, **while**, **before**, and **by the time** to connect events.

### Materials

- The article *Past Tenses for Storytelling*
- The students' coursebook
- Board and markers
- Paper or notebooks
- Optional: three marker colors for the three tenses

## 1. Warm-up: No class today—just gossip

**Time:** 5 minutes  
**Interaction:** Teacher–students

Begin by acknowledging the students' exam week:

> You must be tired after exam week, so I think we need a break. What do you think if we don't have class today? Let's just gossip. I want to tell you the story of how I learned English.

Let students react naturally. Then ask two quick questions to activate the past tense without explaining grammar yet:

- What did you do after your last exam?
- Were you studying late last night?

Accept short answers and keep this stage relaxed. Tell students to listen to your story because you will ask them what they remember afterward.

## 2. Story: How I learned English

**Time:** 10 minutes  
**Interaction:** Teacher–students

Tell the story naturally instead of reading it word for word. Pause occasionally, use gestures, and invite predictions such as “What do you think happened next?” The story below is a polished guide.

> Everything started when I was in my last year of middle school. It was a normal Tuesday, and, as always, I went to my English class.
>
> While I was taking the class, our teacher said, “Guys, I have good news for you.” She told us that some students from the United States were coming to our city. They were studying Spanish, and she was going to select some of her best English students to meet them. Of course, I was selected.
>
> The special day arrived, and I was very nervous. My classmates and I spent the day with the U.S. students. We played soccer, ate guacamayas, and took a trip to Guanajuato City.
>
> While we were traveling to Guanajuato, I noticed a girl named Sophie. She had curly red hair and a beautiful smile. I wanted to talk to her, but I became very nervous.
>
> I tried to speak to her, but I couldn't say what I wanted to say. I had thought that I was good at English, but that experience showed me that I still had a lot to learn. Fortunately for me, Sophie had already learned some Spanish, so we were able to communicate.
>
> That experience left a mark on me. I wanted to speak English more than ever, so I set myself the goal of speaking it fluently one day.
>
> I spent most of my free time studying. Sometimes it was difficult, and I often wondered, “Will I really be able to learn English?” However, I never gave up. I kept studying whenever I had free time.
>
> In the end, the effort paid off. By the time I finished college, I had already taken the TOEFL. What began as one nervous conversation had changed the direction of my life.

After the story, ask simple comprehension questions:

- Where was I when the story began?
- What were we doing when I saw Sophie?
- Why couldn't I talk to her easily?
- What had Sophie learned before the trip?
- What had I done by the time I finished college?

Do not correct every mistake in the answers. The purpose is to confirm that students understood the story and noticed the sequence of events.

## 3. Transition: One story, three jobs

**Time:** 3 minutes  
**Interaction:** Teacher–students

Write these sentences from the story on the board:

1. We **traveled** to Guanajuato.
2. We **were traveling** to Guanajuato when I saw Sophie.
3. Sophie **had learned** some Spanish before the trip.

Ask students what is different about the highlighted verbs. Explain that all three refer to the past, but each one has a different job in the story:

- **traveled** tells a main event;
- **were traveling** shows an action in progress;
- **had learned** takes us further back to an earlier event.

## 4. Grammar explanation: Build the timeline

**Time:** 15 minutes  
**Interaction:** Teacher–students

Use examples from the story and the article. Keep the three model sentences visible while you explain them.

### Simple past: What happened?

Use it for completed actions and the main events that move the story forward.

> Subject + verb in the past

Examples:

- The special day **arrived**.
- We **played** soccer and **ate** guacamayas.
- I **saw** Sophie.

### Past progressive: What was happening?

Use it for the background or an action that was in progress. A simple-past action can happen during or interrupt it.

> Subject + was/were + verb-ing

Write a simple timeline:

> **We were traveling to Guanajuato** ───── ✕ **I saw Sophie.**

Compare the two common patterns:

- **While** we **were traveling**, I **saw** Sophie.
- We **were traveling** **when** I **saw** Sophie.

Emphasize that the progressive action is the longer background action and the simple-past action is the event that advances the story.

### Past perfect: What had happened before?

Use it to make clear that one event happened before another past event.

> Subject + had + past participle

Write a second timeline:

> Sophie **had learned Spanish** → We **met**.

Additional example:

- By the time I **finished** college, I **had taken** the TOEFL.

Explain that students do not need to use the past perfect in every sentence. They should use it when they need to go back and explain an earlier event.

### Board summary

Keep this short guide visible for the rest of the lesson:

| Tense | Job in the story | Basic structure |
|---|---|---|
| Simple past | Main events | subject + past verb |
| Past progressive | Background or action in progress | subject + was/were + verb-ing |
| Past perfect | Earlier past event | subject + had + past participle |

## 5. Controlled practice: Coursebook exercises

**Time:** 12 minutes  
**Interaction:** Individual work, pairs, then whole class

Ask students to complete the assigned exercises about the three past tenses. Before they begin, tell them to ask these questions for each blank:

1. Is this a completed main event? → **simple past**
2. Was this action in progress when another event happened? → **past progressive**
3. Did this happen before another past event? → **past perfect**

Students work individually for the first half of the activity and then compare answers with a partner. During feedback, ask students to explain the function of the tense, not only to give the correct verb form.

If an item combines two actions, draw a quick timeline on the board before confirming the answer.

## 6. Production: Kitchen gossip

**Time:** 12 minutes  
**Interaction:** Groups of five

Divide the class into six groups. Tell students that every kitchen has a story, and each group will invent some harmless kitchen gossip about a service that went wrong.

Assign or let each group choose one situation:

- The chef burned the restaurant's special dish.
- An important ingredient disappeared.
- A server delivered the wrong order.
- A food critic arrived unexpectedly.
- The oven stopped working during dinner service.
- A famous customer entered the restaurant.

Each group creates a story of six to eight sentences. It must include:

- at least three main events in the **simple past**;
- at least one background action in the **past progressive**;
- at least one earlier event in the **past perfect**;
- at least two connectors: **when**, **while**, **before**, or **by the time**.

Display these starters:

- It was a busy evening, and we were...
- While the chef was..., somebody...
- When the customers arrived, ...
- We discovered that somebody had...
- By the time the manager arrived, ...
- In the end, ...

Give each student a role so that a large class remains manageable:

- **Idea creator:** proposes the main problem.
- **Timeline keeper:** decides which event happened first.
- **Writer:** records the story.
- **Grammar checker:** checks the three past tenses.
- **Storyteller:** tells the story to the class.

Each storyteller has up to one minute to share the group's gossip. While listening, the class identifies one example of each tense. If time is limited, have groups tell their stories to a neighboring group and invite two volunteers to present to the class.

Keep the stories fictional and light. Students should not use real classmates' names or share harmful personal rumors.

## 7. Exit ticket

**Time:** 3 minutes  
**Interaction:** Individual

Ask each student to complete these three sentences before leaving:

- Yesterday, I...
- While I was..., ...
- By the time..., I had...

Collect or quickly check the sentences. Use the responses to decide which contrast needs a brief review in the next class.

## Anticipated difficulties

- Students may use the past form after **did**. Remind them: **did + base verb**.
- Students may omit **was/were** in the past progressive. Point back to the board structure.
- Students may write **had + past verb** instead of **had + past participle**. Keep a short list visible: *gone, eaten, seen, taken, made*.
- Students may overuse the past perfect. Ask, “Is there another later past action?” If not, the simple past will usually be enough.
- Students may confuse **when** and **while**. For this lesson, use **while** with the action in progress and **when** with the shorter event.

## Success criteria

Students have met the objective if their final story is understandable and contains:

- a clear sequence of completed events;
- one action that was in progress;
- one event that had happened earlier;
- mostly correct forms of the three target tenses.
